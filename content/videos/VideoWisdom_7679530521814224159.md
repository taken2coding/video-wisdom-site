## Key Insights (Distilled)

1. **Three relations, one hold.** Father/lover/son are tested against the same gesture — the hand reveals the relation more than the blood does.
2. **Gentleness as forensic evidence.** How she holds it disproves "she cut it off herself" — violence would not be gentle, so gentleness argues for motherhood.
3. **Mother holds every dead man as a boy.** The line universalizes maternal holding — all dead men regress to boy in a mother's hands.
4. **Body looking for head vs. head looking for body.** Inversion shows our instinct to give agency to the corpse; correction "Bodies do not look" forces the question to heaven.
5. **Why face heaven?** Burial orientation becomes the clue that even the dead are posed to look — ritual supplies the gaze bodies lack.
6. **Allowed to keep.** The head is not taken but *allowed* — grief is rationed by those who keep the body. Power, not love, decides the relic.
7. **Hollow as canticle.** The hollow (absence of body) is what makes the head sing — the fragment is the whole that power permits.

## Mental Models

- **Hold-as-Diagnosis:** Read relation from grip, not declaration — lover vs. mother is in the hands.

- **Allowed Relic:** What you are allowed to keep reveals who kept the body. Follow the ration, find the power.

## Contrarian Take (Required by ExtractWisdom)

The dialogue's power comes from treating the head as *allowed* relic, but that reading risks romanticizing powerlessness — maybe she *did* cut it off and holds it gently *because* she did, in the way a hunter holds a trophy tenderly exactly because he killed it. The forensic "gentleness proves motherhood" is itself a gentle fiction; real grief is not so legible, and "allowed by whom?" may have no answer — sometimes no one kept the body, and the head is all that remained after time, not power.
