## Summary (1 paragraph)

Miller reframes faith through Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* to answer why delusional confidence like "I can land a 747" persists. Faith, he argues, is not a longer jump requiring more skill or electrolytes — it's an *impossibility* made operative because human impossibility is divine possibility, and divine possibility is certain ("everything impossible is certain"). Via Kierkegaard's broke man who is sure he'll eat and who, knowing nothing about building, leaves a construction site thinking "I'm sure I could manage that," and Abraham who receives Isaac after 100 years only to be told "now kill it," he contrasts faith with skill-moral tests (William Tell's apple-shot, Aeneas' seamanship). Tell and Aeneas succeed by competence; Abraham succeeds by *not* understanding and still acting — his inner slogan the whole walk is "I'm sure it'll be fine / nothing ever happens / it is what it is." Faith is thus not resignation or a calculation of force, but exhaustion through wrestling the infinite (Jacob with Elohim) — a fistfight where you only learn your finitude when you tire, and at that exhaustion human impossibility flips to divine certainty. The punchline: faith in self lets you imagine landing a 747; faith in God lets you imagine nose-diving it into turf and still being fine — because the leap is not hard, it's absurd, and you keep your dream only by being willing to kill it first.

## Key Insights (Distilled)

1. **Faith = absurd impossibility, not difficult possibility.** If you're measuring "how much force can I produce?" you're in long-jump physics, not faith.
2. **Human impossibility ↔ divine possibility.** Kierkegaard's chiasm: what is impossible for you is possible for God, and God's possibility is certain — therefore "everything impossible is certain" for the man of faith.
3. **The broke man's certainty.** Kierkegaard's anecdote: a man with no money is sure he'll eat, sees a building site knowing nothing, leaves sure he could manage it — faith as absurd, ungrounded "I'm sure it'll be fine."
4. **Abraham vs. Agamemnon/William Tell/Aeneas.** Agamemnon sacrifices Iphigenia from tragic necessity; Tell saves his son *by* skill; Aeneas saves his by competence/piety — Abraham saves his *by* killing him. Faith requires no skill, only absurd obedience.
5. **Slogans of the man of faith.** "I'm sure it'll be okay / Nothing ever happens / It is what it is" — the third person of *I Am that I Am*. Mantras that hold the absurd without resolving it.
6. **Leap ≠ long jump.** Long jump is trainable, measurable, electrolyte-dependent; leap of faith is unmeasurable and skill-less — your energy/understanding is categorically insufficient.
7. **Wrestling as epistemology.** You only learn your finitude by wrestling the infinite until exhausted (Jacob/Elohim) — like a fish that must keep moving or die, faith dies if you let go.
8. **747 vs. nose-dive.** Faith in self = delusional competence ("I can land it"); faith in God = delusional surrender ("I can crash it and be fine") — the second is more radical because it abandons self-reliance.

## Mental Models

- **Impossibility Flip:** Human impossible → Divine possible → Certain. Invert every "I can't" through the flip.
- **Skill Test vs. Faith Test:** William Tell/Aeneas (skill/piety) vs. Abraham (faith). Ask: does this require *more competence* or *less understanding*?
- **Long Jump vs. Leap:** Measurable force/electrolytes vs. absurd impossibility. Diagnose which game you're playing.
- **Wrestling the Infinite:** Jacob/Elohim + fish fight — faith is not holding, it's constant wrangling; if you can let go painlessly, it's dead.
- **747 Test:** Land it (self-faith) vs. nose-dive it and be fine (God-faith). Which absurd are you living?
- **Three Slogans:** "I'm sure it'll be okay" / "Nothing ever happens" / "It is what it is" — liturgy for the absurd.

## Actionable Steps

1. **Identify your Tell vs. Abraham:** Write one current "leap" you are treating as a long jump (training, electrolytes, force). Rewrite it as an Abrahamic impossibility: what would "kill it to keep it" look like?
2. **Run the broke-man experiment (today):** Before a task you know nothing about, deliberately say "I'm sure I could manage that" and attempt one small absurd action without researching skill first — notice the shift from competence to faith.
3. **Wrestle 20 minutes:** Pick one impossible that you keep trying to solve with understanding. Instead, wrestle it in prayer/journal as Jacob — don't solve, just exhaust your own understanding until you feel finitude. Then write "human impossibility is divine possibility" and act from that.
4. **Install the slogans:** For 7 days, when anxiety spikes, say aloud the three: "I'm sure it'll be okay. Nothing ever happens. It is what it is." Track how absurd certainty changes behavior vs. anxious calculation.
5. **Fish check:** Where have you let go because the fish stopped moving (faith died quietly)? Re-grip one dead faith this week by re-entering the wrangle — not by trying harder, but by admitting "my energy is not enough."

## Contrarian Take (Required by ExtractWisdom)

Kierkegaard/Miller's celebration of absurd faith risks sanctifying recklessness — "I'm sure it'll be fine" is also how 747s *do* crash, and why Tell practiced for years before shooting. Not every impossibility should be endured as faith; some impossibilities are simply *no* — closed doors, not Abrahamic tests. The missing discernment is *which* impossibility is yours to wrestle: Abraham had a *specific* word ("kill Isaac"), not a vague vibe. Without a word, community, and time-test, "embrace the absurd" becomes spiritual bypass — the fish you're wrestling may not be God, but your own ego refusing to do the skilled, boring work that *is* required (Tell *did* need skill). Faith without competence is as dead as competence without faith.
