## Key Insights (Distilled)

1. **Myrrh for dead and kings — same men, soon.** Dead and king converge quickly; anointing anticipates burial even at birth.
2. **Perfume, not preservative.** Myrrh doesn't preserve what can't be preserved; it perfumes it — ritual is aesthetic, not preventative.
3. **Cruel gift to mothers.** Giving myrrh to a child tells the mother she made "a king, something that would be buried" — knowledge that is cruelty.
4. **All mothers' forbidden body.** Every mother receives a body she is forbidden to keep — motherhood as temporary loan.
5. **Myrrha the tree.** The myrrh-tree birthing Adonis literalizes the unnatural — "Trees do not give birth. Neither should his mother have." She becomes the tree that bleeds perfume.
6. **Everyone wanted him.** Beauty's revenge begins when the beautiful son is universally wanted — desire turns into shame's perfume.
7. **Not a love story.** The interlocutor's hope is corrected: this is not romance but shame — when beauty becomes revenge, fragrance is evidence of wound.

## Contrarian Take (Required by ExtractWisdom)

The canticle's equation of myrrh with shame's revenge risks making every beautiful child a doomed king and every mother a bereaved tree — but some mothers *do* keep their sons, and some beauty does not become revenge, it becomes *home*. Not every myrrh is for burial; Magi's myrrh was also for healing and anointing the living. Without that, the canticle's hollow becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy that beauty must be punished for being wanted.
