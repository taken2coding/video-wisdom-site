## Key Insights (Distilled)

1. **Don't point at me to excuse the city.** Using the voluntary ascetic to justify involuntary suffering is misdirection — my jar is not your city's excuse.
2. **Chose the jar.** The jar is chosen poverty, not fate — agency is the difference.
3. **Voices/hunger not chosen.** The man under the bridge and woman in doorway did not choose their conditions — their suffering is not freedom.
4. **Called all suffering freedom.** Observers collapse chosen poverty and imposed wound into one romantic "freedom" — fools' error.
5. **Weapon vs. wound.** "My poverty is a weapon I hold. Theirs is a wound you learn to step over." — same appearance, opposite structure; one held, one inflicted.
6. **Refuse house vs. cannot find door.** Free man can refuse the house (has house to refuse); broken man cannot find the door (no house to refuse).
7. **Don't use my name to bless neglect.** The final prohibition — don't cite the holy voluntary poor to sanctify systemic neglect.

## Contrarian Take (Required by ExtractWisdom)

The canticle's hard line between chosen jar and unchosen voices risks making voluntary poverty the only legitimate poverty — but most real poverty is mixed, both chosen and unchosen, and the man under the bridge *did* make choices within his voices, as the woman in the doorway makes choices within hunger. "A broken man cannot find the door" can become determinism that denies broken men the very agency the canticle celebrates in the jar-chooser — sometimes the most free act is helping someone find the door they cannot find alone, not just refusing to bless their decay.
