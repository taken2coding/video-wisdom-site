## Key Insights (Distilled)

1. **Gate matters more than prophecy.** Returning from the dead with prophecy is less important than *which* gate you came through — horn (true) vs. ivory (false).
2. **True shades vs. false dreams.** Horn = true shades; ivory = false dreams to sleeping men — same underworld, opposite cargo.
3. **Father's double tour.** City/kings/Caesars/empire (what gods won't end) then the two gates (how gods send dreams) — seeing everything then being shown the exit is the initiation.
4. **Latter gate as prophecy.** Being sent through ivory *creates* the name prophecy — not because it's true, but because men don't check the gate.
5. **Plain-words hiding.** "You don't hide it. You put it right under their nose in plain words, and trust that nobody reads to the end." — concealment via verbosity.
6. **Read to the last line.** The test for your own story: read the one you'd fight for all the way to the end and check its gate. Most stop at the door.

## Contrarian Take (Required by ExtractWisdom)

The canticle's ivory-gate cynicism risks making every prophecy false by definition — if all sleeping men receive false dreams through ivory, then no true shade ever needs to speak, and the only honest move is silence. But Penelope's own test in *Odyssey* is that she *does* ask which gate, and chooses horn — discernment is possible. Not every plain-words prophecy is ivory; some are horn and are called prophecy because they *are* true, not because men didn't check.
