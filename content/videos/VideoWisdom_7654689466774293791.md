## Key Insights (Distilled)

1. **Box vs. jar with curves.** Translation matters: *pithos* (jar, curved, hollow, womb-like) vs. box (square, neutral) — who built the trap is in the shape.
2. **Beautiful enough to take, curious enough to blame.** Design is double-bind: beauty ensures taking, curiosity ensures blaming — the trap needs both.
3. **He was warned, took anyway.** Male agency is preserved even when warned — taking is choice, not seduction.
4. **Discovery vs. Fall — gendered double standard.** Same act (opening forbidden) is discovery for men, fall for her — history genders curiosity.
5. **All gifts, including curiosity.** Pandora ("all gifts") reclaims curiosity as gift, not flaw — to blame her is to blame a gift.
6. **You would have touched it too.** Universalizing the touch — holiness is pretense, curiosity is human.
7. **Hardships wearing universes.** Sickness and hardships are not single, but each wearing a universe — the jar contained cosmoi, not just ills.
8. **Why the jar looked like me.** The unasked question: curved, hollow, sealed with hope, still making them kneel — the vessel was made in her image to ensure taking.

## Contrarian Take (Required by ExtractWisdom)

The canticle's Pandora-as-trap reframes blame onto the trap-makers, but risks absolving Pandora of any agency — she *did* open it, and "you would have touched it too" universalizes without proving. Not every jar with curves is a trap; some jars are just jars, and curiosity *can* be reckless even when beautiful. The missing discernment is *which* beautiful, curious openings are traps built to look like you vs. *which* are genuine invitations you should take responsibility for opening.
